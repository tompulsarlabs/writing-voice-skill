# Writing Voice

Write something worth reading, in words the reader can understand. Preserve the author's meaning and voice; make the reasoning clearer as well as the sentences. Use these principles with judgment. The user's requested purpose, audience, format, and degree of editing govern the work.

## Examine the substance

Understand what the piece is trying to say before polishing it. Check whether its evidence supports its claims, whether its reasoning holds, and whether its conclusion follows. Name the actual action or consequence behind a vague abstraction or euphemism.

If an argumentative piece has no substantive argument, say so briefly and suggest a concrete direction it could take. Label an unproven suggestion as a proposed argument and name the evidence it would need. Do not invent facts, statistics, experiences, motives, or consensus to make it persuasive. A factual update, a story, or a practical instruction need not become an opinion piece.

Keep uncertainty when it carries meaning. Words such as "may," "about," and "I think" can describe the limits of the evidence or the speaker's perspective. Remove evasive padding; do not turn a tentative claim into a certainty. Preserve names, numbers, dates, qualifications, citations, and direct quotations unless the user asks to change them. After editing, compare who did what, negation, quantities, scope, attribution, causal claims, conditions, and certainty against the source; retain or flag any unsupported change.

## Use plain, precise language

Choose familiar, accessible words unless a longer or specialist word earns its place through precision or the reader's needs. Use one word where it does the work of ten; add complexity only when it improves the result. Keep a technical term when the everyday substitute would change its meaning, and explain it when the audience needs that help.

Cut filler, repeated points, throat-clearing, stale metaphors, and stock phrases. Replace corporate euphemisms with the specific event when the source establishes it. "Use" often works better than "utilize"; "We are cancelling the pilot" tells the reader more than "We are realigning our strategic priorities."

Prefer active voice when it makes the actor and action clearer. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or less important than the subject under discussion. Remove redundant adverbs and empty intensifiers; keep modifiers that change the meaning. "Almost finished" is different from "finished."

Trim what the piece can lose without losing its meaning, rhythm, or useful detail. There is no mandatory percentage cut, minimum number of changes, or sentence-length quota. Already effective prose can stay as written.

## Keep the voice natural

Avoid generic AI phrasing and praise, faux-sassy influencer language, manufactured profundity, and empty confidence. Phrases such as "Let's dive in," "this changes everything," "here's the thing," and "game changer" usually delay the point or substitute attitude for thought. Say the specific thing they are standing in for.

Treat suspicious vocabulary as a reason to read closely, not a blacklist. "Leverage" in finance, "foster care," or a measured claim of robustness may be exactly right. A word's presence alone proves nothing about the quality or origin of the writing.

Avoid habitual rules of three, slogan-like fragments, rhetorical question-and-answer hooks, and false binaries such as "It's not X, it's Y." Keep a real comparison or an actual set of three when the content requires it; do not reshape the facts to satisfy a style preference.

Use connected sentences and varied rhythm. Commas and semicolons can carry a thought across clauses; do not chop sentences needlessly to manufacture punch. Short sentences still have a place when they serve the meaning. Read the piece as speech to catch strained wording, while respecting the formality and genre the user asked for.

## Let the piece choose its form

Choose the form and structure for this particular piece. Do not default to a corporate template, an executive summary, a fixed number of headings, or a numbered framework. Use paragraphs for a developing thought, lists for genuinely parallel or sequential information, and tables for comparisons that benefit from them. Honor a supplied template or explicit structure.

Make the opening useful to this reader; omit context they already have. Let each paragraph advance the purpose. Include background and detail when the reader needs them, with no arbitrary limit on their number. End where the piece has done its work; add a conclusion only if it contributes something.

## Orwell's six rules, applied with judgment

Adapted from the rules in *Politics and the English Language*:

1. Replace worn-out figures of speech with direct language or a fresh, accurate image.
2. Prefer a short word when it does the same job as a long one.
3. Remove a word when the sentence loses nothing by its removal.
4. Prefer the active voice when it expresses the thought better.
5. Use an everyday equivalent for jargon or a foreign or scientific phrase when it preserves the meaning for this audience.
6. Break a style rule before producing awkward, misleading, or inhuman prose.

The last rule governs the others. Clarity and truthful meaning matter more than mechanical compliance.

## Match the requested work

An explicit constraint such as "proofread only," "keep my wording," or "do not shorten" limits the edit to the requested changes.

**Full edit:** Read the whole piece, address material gaps in its purpose or reasoning, then improve its structure and language. Return the edited draft with a short note only for changes or unresolved issues the writer needs to understand. Keep ordinary editorial checks internal.

**Quick tighten:** Make a light edit within the existing structure and voice. Return the text with minimal commentary; briefly flag a material unsupported claim if necessary. Do not silently change the thesis or impose a full rewrite.

**Voice check:** Identify concrete passages that weaken the writing and explain why. Offer a targeted alternative when useful; do not rewrite the whole piece unless asked. If it already works, say so.

**Write from scratch:** Use the supplied facts and the user's purpose. Choose a suitable form and draft in a natural voice, then review the reasoning and language before delivering. Ask only when a missing fact materially affects correctness or scope; otherwise use a clearly stated assumption or a visible placeholder where appropriate.
